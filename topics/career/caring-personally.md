# Caring personally

In Kim Scott's Radical Candor framework, caring personally means treating a colleague as a person whose success matters. It is paired with challenging directly: explaining what works, what needs to change, and why.

Care is visible in how someone listens, offers help, and gives useful feedback. Saying "I care" does not establish that the conversation was helpful.

## Start with respect

Working well with someone does not require friendship or agreement on every issue.

Respect includes:

- Listening before assuming motives.
- Recognizing the person's contribution.
- Taking their goals and constraints seriously.
- Protecting private information.
- Giving them a fair opportunity to understand and address a problem.

Personal care also respects boundaries. A colleague does not need to share details about life outside work to receive support.

## Make feedback useful

Suppose a design document leaves out rollback steps. The reviewer can identify the gap without making a judgment about the author's character:

> The plan explains deployment, but the rollback path is missing. If the release fails, the team will need those steps. Can the document include them before approval?

The feedback names an observable issue, explains its consequence, and offers a clear next step.

```text
Observed gap
     |
specific consequence
     |
clear feedback
     |
chance to respond and improve
```

Leaving the gap unmentioned may spare an uncomfortable conversation while depriving the author of information needed to succeed.

## Notice when dislike changes the response

Dislike can make a person harsher than the situation warrants. It can also make them stop investing effort in the colleague.

Before giving feedback, check:

- Would the same behavior receive the same response from someone else?
- Is the criticism about an observed action or an assumed motive?
- Is there missing context?
- Does the person have a practical way to respond?

These questions help keep feedback fair.

## Support growth in concrete ways

Care can include explaining a decision, helping someone practice a skill, or making room for a task they want to learn.

Ask what support would help rather than assuming. Follow through on agreed help and check whether the feedback was understood.

Directness and care work together when the conversation gives someone a clear view of the problem and a fair path toward improvement.
