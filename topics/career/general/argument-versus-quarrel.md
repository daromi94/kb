# Argument versus quarrel

An argument and a quarrel can look identical from outside, yet they
differ on two axes: what they target and what they pursue. An
argument targets an idea to find the truth. A quarrel targets a
person to win status.

## Target: the idea or the person

An argument targets the premises. You examine the structure of a
claim and challenge a specific part of it: the premise is false, or
the conclusion does not follow from it. You attack a piece of the
reasoning, never the reasoner.

A quarrel abandons the reasoning and attacks the speaker. This is the
ad hominem fallacy — Latin for "to the person." Instead of showing
the idea is broken, you try to show the person is foolish,
incompetent, or malicious.

## Goal: truth or status

An argument is positive-sum. Both sides work on the same problem, so
a flaw one side exposes is a flaw both are now rid of. Finding the
fatal flaw in your own reasoning is not a loss — you have eliminated
a bad idea and moved closer to the truth.

A quarrel is zero-sum. The goal is not to solve a problem but to gain
status, and status only transfers: for one person to win, the other
must lose face.

## The contrast in practice

A team is deciding whether to delay a launch.

The argument targets the risk and seeks the safest decision:

> I disagree with launching Friday. Peak load hits over the weekend,
> when the support team is offline. Can the infrastructure absorb a
> 40% spike unmonitored?

The quarrel targets the person and asserts dominance:

> You always want to delay launches because you are too risk-averse,
> and your team misses deadlines anyway.

## Trust sustains hard disagreement

Blunt, sustained disagreement is possible only when both sides trust
that the exchange is an argument over the truth, not a quarrel aimed
at the person. When someone finds a deep flaw in your position, you
can take it impersonally because you trust the target was the idea,
not you.
