# Validity versus soundness

A deductive argument can be logically flawless yet reach a false
conclusion. Two separate properties decide whether to trust it:
validity asks whether the conclusion follows from the premises;
soundness asks whether those premises are also true.

## Validity: does the conclusion follow

An argument is valid when it is impossible for its premises to be
true and its conclusion false. Validity depends only on whether the
conclusion follows, not on whether the premises are actually true.
"All birds can fly; a penguin is a bird; therefore a penguin can
fly" is valid — the conclusion follows — yet false.

## Soundness: are the premises true

An argument is sound when it is valid and all of its premises are
actually true. Soundness is the higher bar: it demands correct
reasoning and correct facts together. A sound argument cannot reach
a false conclusion.

## Why the distinction matters

You can check validity from the inside, by inspecting the reasoning
alone. You can check soundness only against the outside world, by
testing whether the premises hold. This is why an argument can feel
unassailable yet be wrong: the logic is easy to confirm, the
premises are not, so the flaw sits in a premise you never tested.
